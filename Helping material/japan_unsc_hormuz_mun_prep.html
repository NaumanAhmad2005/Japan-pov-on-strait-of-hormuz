Why Japan cares — existential stakes
  • Japan imports approximately 90% of its crude oil through the Strait of Hormuz — any closure or disruption directly threatens its industrial economy.
  • Japan is the world's largest LNG importer; Qatar and the UAE supply a huge chunk via Hormuz.
  • The 2019 tanker attacks near Hormuz (including on a Japanese-operated vessel, Kokuka Courageous) made Japan directly aggrieved.
  • Japan has dispatched MSDF (Maritime Self-Defense Force) vessels to the region for intelligence gathering since 2020.
  • Economic disruption from a Hormuz closure is estimated to cost Japan tens of billions per month — equivalent to shutting down major industries.
Japan's foreign policy DNA
  • Pacifist constitution (Article 9) — Japan cannot engage in offensive military action; it must frame all engagement as "peace-keeping" or "diplomacy-first."
  • Japan maintains a historically unique dual relationship with both the US and Iran — it was the only major US ally that did NOT immediately end oil imports from Iran after US sanctions in 2018 (it got a waiver).
  • PM Abe visited Tehran in 2019 as a mediator — Japan positioned itself as a neutral peace broker between Iran and the West.
  • Japan strongly supports UNCLOS (UN Convention on the Law of the Sea) — freedom of navigation is a core legal pillar.
  • Japan co-leads with the EU on multilateral dialogue — prefers the JCPOA revival framework over military escalation.
Key facts about the Strait of Hormuz
  • Only 33 km wide at its narrowest point; 2 shipping lanes each just 3.2 km wide.
  • Roughly 20–21 million barrels of oil per day pass through (~21% of global petroleum trade).
  • Iran has repeatedly threatened to close the strait in response to sanctions or military action.
  • Key actors: Iran (northern shore), Oman (southern shore), UAE, US Navy (5th Fleet in Bahrain).
  • Tensions flashpoints: drone attacks, tanker seizures, IRGC naval provocations, Houthi spillover from Yemen.
  • Operation Sentinel / IMSC (International Maritime Security Construct) — US-led coalition Japan did NOT join (to preserve Iran relations); instead Japan formed its own independent surveillance mission.
Japan's position on key issues
  • On Iran sanctions: Supports targeted sanctions via UNSC, opposes unilateral US sanctions that destabilize diplomacy.
  • On JCPOA: Strongly favors revival; nuclear deal reduces the core driver of tension.
  • On militarization: Opposes any military escalation; supports diplomatic back-channels.
  • On freedom of navigation: Absolute supporter — cites UNCLOS Article 38 (transit passage rights).
  • On multilateralism: Prefers any solution to go through UNSC or IAEA, not unilateral action.

Opening Speech — General Speakers List (90 sec)

Tone & strategy
Diplomatic Multilateral Energy-Security framing Non-confrontational

Establish Japan as: (1) the most economically affected non-party, (2) a credible peace broker with Iran AND the West, (3) champion of UNCLOS and maritime law. Set up your resolution pillars early.

"Honorable Chair, distinguished delegates,

The Strait of Hormuz is not merely a waterway. It is the lifeline of the global economy — and for Japan, it is existential. Nearly ninety percent of Japan's crude oil, and the vast majority of our liquefied natural gas, passes through those thirty-three kilometers of water every single day. When this strait is threatened, Japan does not merely observe — Japan suffers.

Japan has experienced this reality firsthand. In June 2019, a Japanese-operated tanker was struck in the Gulf of Oman. Yet even in that moment, Japan's response was not military — it was diplomatic. Prime Minister Abe flew to Tehran not with ultimatums, but with dialogue. Because Japan understands what this Council must also understand: there is no military solution to the Strait of Hormuz crisis. There is only a diplomatic one.

Japan calls upon this Council to pursue three pillars: first, the unconditional protection of freedom of navigation under UNCLOS; second, the revival of multilateral diplomatic frameworks including the JCPOA; and third, the establishment of a permanent UNSC maritime monitoring mechanism for the Persian Gulf region.

Japan stands ready to work with all delegations — east and west, north and south — to ensure that the Strait of Hormuz remains open, lawful, and peaceful. The world cannot afford otherwise. Japan will not allow otherwise.

Thank you, Honorable Chair."

Moderated Caucus 1 — Freedom of Navigation & Legal Framework

Suggested motion to raise

"Japan moves for a moderated caucus of 8 minutes, with 60 seconds per speaker, on the topic: Legal frameworks for protecting freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz."

Your 60-second speech
"Honorable Chair,

Under Article 38 of UNCLOS, all ships — commercial, civilian, military — enjoy the right of transit passage through international straits. This is not a privilege. It is international law.

Iran's repeated threats to close the Strait, and the pattern of tanker seizures by the IRGC — most recently in 2023 — constitute direct violations of these legal norms. Japan, as the single most economically dependent nation on this waterway, urges this Council to reaffirm UNCLOS as the binding framework.

We propose the Council pass a resolution explicitly invoking Chapter VI of the UN Charter, calling on all parties to respect transit passage rights, and establishing a UNSC-mandated maritime observation mission — independent of any single nation's command — to monitor compliance in real time.

Japan is not seeking confrontation with any state. Japan is seeking the rule of law. Because when the law fails in the Strait of Hormuz, every nation in this room pays the price at the pump, at the factory, and at the dinner table.

Thank you."
Key arguments to build on in debate
  • UNCLOS has 168 state parties — it is arguably the most universally ratified treaty in international law. Iran IS a signatory.
  • Transit passage rights (UNCLOS Art. 38–39) cannot be suspended even in wartime under customary international law.
  • UNSC Resolution 598 (Iran-Iraq war) already set precedent for UNSC intervention in Gulf maritime security.
  • Japan's independent MSDF deployment demonstrates that non-confrontational maritime presence is possible and legal.
  • A UNSC monitoring body is different from a military coalition — it has legitimacy, neutrality, and legal authority.
Counter arguments & rebuttals
  • If Iran/Russia says "Western interference": "Japan's proposal is through the UNSC — the most legitimate multilateral body. Japan itself chose NOT to join the US-led Operation Sentinel. This is precisely to ensure neutrality."
  • If someone says UNCLOS doesn't apply to military vessels: "Articles 38-40 apply to ALL ships including warships in international straits — this is settled law."
  • If USA pushes for military enforcement: "Japan urges restraint. Unilateral military action risks the very destabilization we seek to prevent."

Moderated Caucus 2 — Iran, Sanctions & Diplomatic De-escalation

Suggested motion to raise

"Japan moves for a moderated caucus of 10 minutes, with 75 seconds per speaker, on the topic: Diplomatic pathways and the role of sanctions in resolving tensions in the Strait of Hormuz."

Your 75-second speech
"Honorable Chair,

Sanctions without diplomacy are a weapon without a target. Japan has seen this repeatedly in the Persian Gulf context.

The 2015 JCPOA demonstrated that multilateral diplomatic engagement CAN constrain Iran's nuclear program. Its collapse in 2018 — not through Iranian violation, but through unilateral US withdrawal — directly contributed to the escalation we now debate. Japan never abandoned its diplomatic relationship with Tehran precisely because we understood that coercion alone would not work.

Japan proposes that this Council formally endorse the resumption of JCPOA negotiations, tie any further sanctions to specific, verifiable Iranian actions — not geopolitical pressure — and establish a UN Special Envoy for Persian Gulf Security Dialogue, modeled on successful precedents in the Korean Peninsula and Afghanistan.

Japan has served as an intermediary between Iran and the international community. We are willing to host or co-host such dialogue. We call on Iran to return to the table, and we call on all P5 members to prioritize a negotiated solution over military posturing.

The Strait of Hormuz will only be truly secure when Iran feels secure. That requires engagement — not encirclement.

Thank you, Chair."
Key arguments to build on
  • Iran's motivation to threaten Hormuz is directly tied to sanctions pressure and security fears — address the cause, not just the symptom.
  • Japan's unique position: only major US ally that maintained dialogue with Iran post-2018 sanctions. This gives Japan credibility with BOTH sides.
  • JCPOA: Iran was verified compliant by the IAEA for years before the US withdrew. Revival is possible.
  • UN Special Envoy model: successful in Yemen ceasefire talks — same structure could work for Gulf security.
  • Multilateral sanctions (UNSC-authorized) are more effective and more legitimate than unilateral sanctions — make this distinction clearly.
Counter arguments & rebuttals
  • If USA/UK says Iran violated JCPOA: "Iran's violations began AFTER the US withdrawal in 2018 and AFTER Europe failed to provide promised sanctions relief. The sequence matters for accountability."
  • If hardliners say diplomacy failed: "Diplomacy was abandoned, not failed. The IAEA verified Iranian compliance for years. We owe it to global stability to try again."
  • If China/Russia vetoes security measures: Find common ground — both China and Russia support JCPOA revival. Build a bloc here.

Moderated Caucus 3 — Long-term Maritime Security Architecture

Suggested motion to raise

"Japan moves for a moderated caucus of 12 minutes, with 90 seconds per speaker, on the topic: Building a permanent multilateral maritime security architecture for the Persian Gulf."

Your 90-second speech
"Honorable Chair,

This Council has reacted to crises in the Strait of Hormuz for decades. It is time we stopped reacting and started building.

Japan proposes the creation of a Persian Gulf Maritime Safety and Cooperation Framework — a permanent, UNSC-mandated institution with four components:

First, a Joint Maritime Incident Room, co-staffed by regional and international observers, to provide real-time transparency and prevent miscalculation from escalating into conflict — similar to the Communication and Coordination Agreement already operating between Israel and Russia in Syrian airspace.

Second, an independent UN Maritime Monitor deployed in the strait to document compliance with UNCLOS, neutrally — not under any single great power's command.

Third, an Emergency Economic Stabilization Protocol — pre-agreed mechanisms for releasing strategic petroleum reserves coordinated by the IEA — so that any temporary disruption does not cause global economic panic. Japan, the US, and IEA members already coordinate on this; we propose formalizing it under UN auspices.

Fourth, a Confidence-Building Measures program between Iran and GCC states, facilitated by a neutral party — Japan offers itself for this role — modeled on the CSCE mechanisms that reduced Cold War tensions in Europe.

We cannot solve Hormuz with resolutions alone. We need architecture. Japan is ready to build it.

Thank you, Chair."
Resolution clause ideas (for your draft)
  • PP clause: "Recalling UNSC Resolution 598 (1987) and its precedent for Council authority over Gulf maritime security,"
  • PP clause: "Deeply concerned by the pattern of tanker seizures and navigational harassment in the Strait of Hormuz since 2019,"
  • OP 1: "Reaffirms the right of transit passage under UNCLOS Article 38 as inviolable international law;"
  • OP 2: "Calls upon all parties to refrain from any action that threatens, harasses, or impedes commercial navigation in the Strait of Hormuz;"
  • OP 3: "Establishes a UN Persian Gulf Maritime Monitoring Mission (UNPMM) with a mandate of 18 months, to be reviewed by the Council;"
  • OP 4: "Urges the resumption of multilateral diplomatic negotiations on Iran's nuclear program under IAEA verification;"
  • OP 5: "Encourages member states to activate IEA emergency petroleum reserve protocols in the event of navigational disruption;"

"Japan moves for an unmoderated caucus of 15 minutes for the purpose of working paper drafting and bloc formation."

Move unmod when: you have a draft ready to show; you need to flip a swing vote; you want to merge two competing papers; or when debate is going in circles and you need to reset.

🇮🇷 Approaching the Iran delegation (if present)
"Japan has never treated Iran as an adversary. We maintained diplomatic relations when others did not. Our proposal does not target Iran — it protects a global commons. Japan would welcome Iran's input on the monitoring mechanism's mandate to ensure it respects Iranian sovereignty."

Offer Iran a face-saving clause: monitoring is of ALL vessels, not just Iranian military — parity framing.

📄 Working paper structure (propose this)
  • Section A: Reaffirmation of UNCLOS (easy consensus, everyone signs)
  • Section B: Diplomatic pathway — JCPOA, UN Special Envoy (China/Russia win here)
  • Section C: Maritime Monitoring Mission (your flagship — link to Section A for legal grounding)
  • Section D: Economic Stabilization Protocol — IEA coordination (keep it soft, non-binding to get more signatories)
⚡ Power move: Merge before voting

If another bloc has a competing paper, propose a merger. In UNSC, you only need 9 votes and no P5 veto. A merged paper with US + China + Japan as co-sponsors is almost unvetable. Sacrifice minor clauses to get the big ones through.
