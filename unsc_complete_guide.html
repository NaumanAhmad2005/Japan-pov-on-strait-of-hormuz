What is it?

The UN Security Council

The most powerful body in international law

The UNSC is the only UN organ whose decisions are legally binding on all 193 UN member states. While the General Assembly debates and recommends, the Security Council acts. It can authorize military force, impose sanctions, establish peacekeeping missions, and refer cases to the International Criminal Court. No other international body has this authority.

Its primary mandate

Under the UN Charter (Chapter V–VII), the UNSC holds primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security. When the Council determines that a situation constitutes a "threat to the peace, breach of the peace, or act of aggression" (Article 39), it can take binding action under:

Chapter VI — Peaceful Settlement Chapter VII — Enforcement Action Chapter VIII — Regional Arrangements

Why UNSC simulation is different from GA-MUN

  • Smaller and more intense — only 15 delegations, every delegate speaks frequently
  • Vetoes are real — one P5 member can kill any resolution
  • More formal procedure — the President (Chair) has far more control over debate
  • Higher political stakes — positions must be consistent with real state policy
  • Resolutions are binding — in real life, the world must comply
Who sits at the table?

Council Composition

🔴 Permanent Members (P5) — The Veto Holders

These 5 nations hold permanent seats and the power of veto. They were the victorious Allied powers of World War II.

🇺🇸 United States 🇬🇧 United Kingdom 🇫🇷 France 🇷🇺 Russia 🇨🇳 China

A single NO vote from any P5 member on a substantive matter = the resolution dies. No appeal, no override.

🔵 Elected Members (E10) — Two-Year Terms

Ten non-permanent members elected by the GA for 2-year terms. They rotate based on regional groups and have no veto power but their votes are essential — you still need 9 total affirmative votes.

💡
In your simulation, the 24 countries listed include both P5 members and non-P5 states. In a real UNSC only 15 sit. Your sim has expanded membership — treat everyone with the same procedural rules but remember only the real P5 can veto.

The President of the Council

The presidency rotates monthly among all 15 members in alphabetical order. The President conducts sessions, recognizes speakers, puts motions to a vote, and manages debate. In simulations, this role is played by the Chair/Director. You address them as:

"Mr. / Madam President" — not "Honorable Chair" (that's GA language)
From start to finish

The Complete Session Flow

1
Opening — Roll Call & Quorum

The President calls the session to order and conducts a roll call. Each delegation responds "Present" or "Present and Voting." "Present and Voting" means you cannot abstain later. A quorum (majority of members) must be established before any business can proceed. The agenda is formally adopted — usually by acclamation or a simple procedural vote.

2
Setting the Agenda

If there is more than one item, a motion is made to set the order of business. In most simulations with one agenda item (e.g., Strait of Hormuz), this is done automatically. The President announces: "The Council will now consider the item on its agenda: [topic]."

3
General Speakers List (GSL) Opens

The President opens the General Speakers List. Delegates raise their placards and the President adds them to the list. Each delegation delivers an opening speech (typically 60–120 seconds) stating their country's position. This is your first chance to plant your flag, establish your stance, and signal potential alliances. The GSL continues throughout the session as a fallback between other modes of debate.

4
Moderated Caucus (Focused Debate)

Any delegate can motion for a moderated caucus on a specific topic. If passed, the President recognizes speakers one by one for short speeches. This is the main engine of formal debate. Multiple moderated caucuses occur throughout the session, each focused on a different subtopic. Most of your prepared speeches will be delivered here.

5
Unmoderated Caucus (Informal Negotiation)

The session is suspended. Delegates leave their seats, move around freely, and negotiate. This is where alliances are built, working papers are drafted, compromises are made, and blocs are formed. No formal record is kept. This is the most important part of UNSC — the real diplomacy happens here. Use every second wisely.

6
Working Paper Stage

During or after unmod, delegations submit a Working Paper — an informal draft resolution with preambulatory and operative clauses. It doesn't have binding status yet. The President acknowledges it. Other delegations can sign as signatories (support debate) or co-sponsors (endorse the content). Multiple competing working papers may exist simultaneously.

7
Draft Resolution Stage

A working paper is upgraded to a Draft Resolution when it is formally introduced on the floor and accepted by the President. It receives an official number (e.g., S/2026/DR/1). From this point, it is read aloud or distributed, debated in moderated caucuses, and can be amended. This is the document that will be voted on.

8
Amendments

Any delegation can propose a Friendly Amendment (accepted by the main sponsors — no vote needed) or an Unfriendly Amendment (opposed by sponsors — requires a simple majority vote to be incorporated). Amendments change specific clauses in the draft resolution before the final vote.

9
Closing Debate & Voting Procedure

A delegate motions to "close debate" on the draft resolution. This is voted on — requires a simple majority. If debate closes, the Council moves immediately into Voting Procedure. During voting procedure, no one may speak, leave, or interrupt until all votes are recorded. The President calls each delegation and records Yes / No / Abstain.

10
Explanation of Vote (EOV)

After voting, delegations may request the floor for an Explanation of Vote — a brief statement explaining why they voted the way they did. This is especially important if a P5 member vetoed, or if a country voted against its usual alliance. It's a diplomatic face-saving mechanism and a rhetorical opportunity.

11
Resolution Adopted or Vetoed

If the resolution receives 9+ affirmative votes AND no P5 veto, it passes and becomes an official Security Council Resolution. If a P5 member votes NO — it is vetoed and fails entirely, regardless of how many others voted yes. The session may then continue with debate on a new or amended draft.

How to move

All Procedural Motions

⚠️
In UNSC, always get the floor first: raise your placard and wait for the President to say "The Chair recognizes [Country]." Then make your motion. Never speak without being recognized.
🎙
Request to Speak (Floor Request)
Any time

The most basic action. Before ANY speech or motion, you must be recognized.

"The delegation of [Country] requests the floor."
📋
Moderated Caucus
Formal debate

Opens a structured, Chair-moderated debate on a specific subtopic. Delegates are recognized one by one. The most-used mode in UNSC. Requires a simple majority to pass.

"The delegation of [Country] motions for a moderated caucus of [total time], with [X] seconds per speaker, on the topic of [specific topic]."
🤝
Unmoderated Caucus
Informal negotiation

Suspends formal session. Delegates move freely to negotiate, draft, and build blocs. No formal record. Essential for passing any resolution. Requires a simple majority to pass.

"The delegation of [Country] motions for an unmoderated caucus of [time] for the purpose of [working paper drafting / bloc consultation / etc.]."
⏱
Extension of Caucus
Time extension

When a caucus is running out of time and debate is unfinished. Made immediately before time expires.

"The delegation of [Country] motions to extend the current caucus by [time]."
📄
Introduction of Working Paper
Document

Formally places your working paper on the floor. State co-sponsors when introducing.

"The delegation of [Country] wishes to introduce Working Paper [number], co-sponsored by [Country A], [Country B], and [Country C]."
✏️
Friendly Amendment
No vote needed

Proposed change to a draft resolution that the main sponsors accept. No vote required — automatically incorporated.

"[Country] proposes a friendly amendment to operative clause [X]: [new language]. The main sponsors have signaled their acceptance."
✏️
Unfriendly Amendment
Requires majority vote

Proposed change that sponsors oppose. Goes to a vote — simple majority of present members needed to incorporate it.

"[Country] moves to amend operative clause [X] to read: [new language]." → Vote called immediately.
🔒
Motion to Close Debate
Moves to vote

Ends debate on the current draft resolution and immediately triggers voting procedure. Requires a simple majority. This is how you bring a resolution to its final vote.

"The delegation of [Country] motions to close debate on Draft Resolution [number] and move into voting procedure."
📊
Division of the Question
Advanced

Request to vote on individual clauses of a resolution separately rather than as a whole. Useful when you support some clauses but not others. Rarely used in simulations.

"The delegation of [Country] moves for division of the question, requesting a separate vote on operative clause [X]."
⏸
Motion to Table / Postpone
Advanced

Temporarily suspends consideration of a working paper or motion without killing it. The document remains available for future consideration.

"The delegation of [Country] moves to table Working Paper [number] pending further consultation."
Interruptions & inquiries

All Types of Points

⚠️
Points interrupt the current flow of business. Some can interrupt a speaker mid-speech. Use them precisely — overusing points damages your credibility with the Chair and other delegates.
🛑

Point of Order

Interrupts speaker

Used when a procedural rule is being violated. This is the most powerful point — it can interrupt a delegate mid-speech. Examples: a delegate exceeding their speaking time, speaking to a topic outside the agenda, or the Chair making a procedural error.

"Point of Order, Mr./Madam President — the delegate's remarks appear to exceed the scope of the current agenda item."
⚡
Do NOT abuse this. Raising a bogus Point of Order to silence a strong speaker will backfire. The Chair can rule it out of order immediately.
❓

Point of Inquiry / Parliamentary Inquiry

Asks the Chair

A question directed to the Chair/President about procedure or the rules of the committee. Cannot interrupt a speaker.

"Point of Parliamentary Inquiry, Madam President — may the delegation request clarification on the current voting threshold for this draft resolution?"
💬

Point of Information

Asks a delegate

A question directed to another delegate, asked after their speech concludes (not during). The targeted delegate may choose to yield or decline. This is your main tool for putting other delegations on the defensive.

"Point of Information directed to the delegate of Iran — will the delegate clarify how their delegation reconciles its stated commitment to multilateralism with the unilateral seizure of commercial vessels in violation of UNCLOS Article 38?"
💡
Make your POIs sharp and specific. Vague questions get vague answers. A well-aimed POI puts the other delegate on the record with a difficult question.
🔁

Right of Reply

After your country is attacked

Used when another delegation makes a personal attack on your country or misrepresents your position. Must be requested immediately after the offending speech. The Chair grants it at their discretion. Keep it brief (30–45 seconds) and factual — do not use it for additional speech time.

"The delegation of Japan requests the Right of Reply."
[Chair grants it]
"The delegate of [X] has mischaracterized Japan's position. Japan explicitly chose NOT to join the US-led Operation Sentinel — a documented fact that directly contradicts the delegate's claim."
📢

Point of Personal Privilege

Physical discomfort

Used when a delegate cannot fully participate due to a physical issue — can't hear the speaker, room temperature, technical issues with their microphone, etc. Can interrupt a speaker. This is not for debate — using it for anything else is a procedural violation.

"Point of Personal Privilege — the delegation is unable to hear the speaker from this position."
Modes of debate

Types of Debate in UNSC

🎙 General Speakers List (GSL)

FormalOpening statements90–120 sec

The default mode of debate. The Chair opens a list and delegates sign up. Speeches are general position statements. The GSL is always running in the background — when no caucus is active, debate returns to the GSL. Think of it as your opening argument: establish identity, plant your pillars, signal alliances.

Typical GSL structure:
Sentence 1–2: Frame the issue through your country's lens
Sentence 3–4: State your national stake
Sentence 5–7: Announce your policy position
Sentence 8–9: Propose your framework / call to action

📋 Moderated Caucus

FormalFocused topic45–90 sec per speaker

The workhorse of UNSC debate. A specific topic is set by the motion. The Chair recognizes speakers one at a time. This is where your prepared speeches go. Each moderated caucus should advance a specific argument pillar. Motion for one when you have a focused argument to make or when you want to steer debate toward your agenda.

💡
Strategy tip: The delegate who MOTIONS for a moderated caucus sets the topic — this is enormous power. Frame the topic in language that favors your position. "On the legal obligations of littoral states under UNCLOS" vs. "On security threats in the Persian Gulf" are the same issue but frame completely differently.

🤝 Unmoderated Caucus

InformalNo record15–20 min

Session is suspended. This is where resolutions are actually built. Use this time to: draft working paper language with allies, make private concessions to swing votes, pitch a co-sponsorship deal to China, warn the USA against military language, and neutralize opposition. Every unmod should have a specific target and outcome. Don't wander.

Unmod checklist for Japan:
□ Approach S. Korea/India first — confirm co-sponsorship
□ Private offer to China — JCPOA clause trade
□ Concession to Saudi — tanker seizure language
□ Red line to USA — no military authorization clause
□ Soft pitch to Oman — mediator role endorsement

📢 Yield Options (After a Speech)

After finishing a speech, a delegate may yield their remaining time in three ways:

1

Yield to another delegate

"Japan yields the floor to the delegation of South Korea." — That delegate gets your remaining time.

2

Yield to Points of Information

"Japan yields to Points of Information." — Other delegates can now ask you questions. Shows confidence.

3

Yield to the Chair

"Japan yields to the Chair." — Remaining time is forfeited. Most common ending.

What you write and submit

Documents in UNSC

1

Position Paper

Written before the conference. States your country's official position on the agenda item. Establishes your stance before you even enter the chamber. Usually 1–2 pages. In your case — Japan's position paper you just received.

2

Working Paper

An informal draft resolution written during the conference. Not yet in resolution format. May have signatories (supporters) and co-sponsors (endorsers). Multiple working papers can exist simultaneously. Submitted to the dais for acknowledgment — not voted on directly.

3

Draft Resolution

A working paper that has been formally introduced on the floor, formatted correctly (PP + OP clauses), accepted by the President, and assigned an official number. This is the document that goes to a vote.

4

Resolution

A draft resolution that has passed the vote (9+ yes, no P5 veto). In real UNSC it is legally binding on all 193 UN member states. In simulation it represents the committee's final output.

5

Amendment

A proposed change to a specific clause of a draft resolution. Friendly amendments (accepted by sponsors) need no vote. Unfriendly amendments (rejected by sponsors) go to a simple majority vote.

Resolution Structure — PP and OP Clauses

Every UNSC resolution is divided into two parts:

Preambulatory Clauses (PP)

Begin with past-tense verbs. Provide context, cite precedents, acknowledge the situation. Not binding.

Examples: Recalling… Reaffirming… Deeply concerned by… Having considered… Noting with appreciation…

Operative Clauses (OP)

Begin with present-tense verbs. State what the Council actually DOES or CALLS UPON others to do. These are the binding actions.

Examples: Decides… Demands… Calls upon… Authorizes… Establishes… Urges… Encourages…

⚡
"Decides" is the strongest operative verb — legally binding. "Urges" is the weakest — merely recommendatory. Choose your verbs strategically based on what you can get passed through a P5 veto.
The final step

Voting Procedure

⚠️ The UNSC Threshold — Different from Everything Else

9 Affirmative Votes Required Zero P5 Negative Votes

This is the single most important rule. Out of 15 members, you need at least 9 yes votes. But even if you get all 14 other votes, a single NO from any P5 member kills the resolution. Abstentions by P5 members do NOT constitute a veto.

EXAMPLE: Resolution passes with 11-2-2

ABSTAIN (2)
🇷🇺 Russia ★P5 — abstains (NOT a veto) 🇨🇳 China ★P5 — abstains
NO (2)
🇮🇷 Iran 🇸🇾 Syria

✅ RESOLUTION PASSES — 9+ yes, no P5 negative vote

EXAMPLE: Resolution VETOED despite 14 yes votes

14 countries vote YES 🇷🇺 Russia votes NO ★VETO

❌ RESOLUTION FAILS — one P5 negative vote kills it entirely

Roll Call Vote vs. Placard Vote

The Chair may call the vote in two ways:

  • Placard Vote: Quick — delegates raise placards for YES, NO, ABSTAIN. Used for procedural motions.
  • Roll Call Vote: Formal — each delegation is called by name and states their vote aloud. Used for substantive resolutions. This is what the final vote on a draft resolution looks like.

Present vs. Present and Voting

During roll call at the start of session, how you respond matters:

  • "Present" — you may vote Yes, No, OR Abstain
  • "Present and Voting" — you commit to voting Yes or No only. You cannot abstain.

For Japan's position — respond "Present" to preserve the abstention option as a diplomatic tool if needed.

The nuclear option

The P5 Veto — Complete Guide

What is the Veto?

Under Article 27(3) of the UN Charter, any substantive resolution requires the "concurring votes" of all five permanent members. In practice, this means a single NO vote from any P5 member — USA, UK, France, Russia, or China — defeats the resolution permanently. The veto has no override mechanism. There is no appeal.

Substantive vs. Procedural Matters

Substantive (veto applies)

Resolutions authorizing action, sanctions, peacekeeping missions, ceasefire demands, ICC referrals. Anything that makes a decision affecting a state's sovereignty or behavior.

Procedural (no veto)

Motions to hold a meeting, set the agenda, invite non-member states to speak, or other housekeeping matters. Only simple majority needed — P5 cannot veto these.

⚠️
The "double veto" — A P5 member can veto the characterization of a matter as procedural, effectively vetoing the procedural vote itself. This is controversial but technically possible.

Veto History & Real-World Context

  • Russia has used the veto 140+ times — more than any other P5 member
  • USA has used it 80+ times — mostly to protect Israel from UNSC action
  • China and Russia have jointly vetoed resolutions on Syria 16 times
  • In 2022, Russia vetoed a resolution condemning its invasion of Ukraine
  • The UK and France have not used the veto since 1989
  • On Strait of Hormuz: Russia and China are the veto threats for any Western-leaning resolution

How to Work Around a Veto (In Simulation)

  • Soften the operative verbs: Change "Demands" → "Calls upon" → "Encourages" until the objecting P5 member can abstain instead of vote no
  • Give the P5 member a win: Include a clause that reflects their stated position — Russia gets "anti-unilateral sanctions" language, China gets "JCPOA co-authorship" credit
  • Split the resolution: Pass the uncontroversial parts (UNCLOS reaffirmation) separately from the controversial ones (monitoring mission)
  • Private diplomacy: In unmod, make the P5 member an explicit offer — what do they need to abstain instead of veto?
  • The Uniting for Peace precedent: If UNSC deadlocks due to veto, an emergency special session of the GA can be called under UNGA Res. 377 — not available in UNSC sim but good to know

Even a Vetoed Resolution Can Be a Win (In MUN)

If your resolution gets 11+ votes but is vetoed by Russia, you have demonstrated that:

  • Your coalition-building was successful
  • Your resolution was balanced and persuasive
  • Russia/China isolated themselves and took the blame
  • You showed leadership even without a passage

In MUN awards, chairs evaluate leadership, diplomacy, and speech quality — not just whether your resolution passed. A well-built vetoed resolution often wins more awards than a weak resolution that barely scraped 9 votes.
